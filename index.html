of Work

By Noah Steingart, Koen Iyer, Caleb McFarlane, and Juliana Silva Reis

Work is changing quickly. Where people work, the tools they use, what new employees expect from their employers, and how organizations protect the health of their people are all being rethought at the same time. This portfolio looks at those changes through the lens of organizational behaviour.

Flexibility: Remote and Hybrid Work

By Koen Iyer

Corporate Culture: Dropbox, "Virtual First"

Source and type
Corporate culture. Dropbox Blog (company announcement) and MIT Sloan School of Management (business school article)
Author
Dropbox; M. Somers
Date
October 13, 2020; October 27, 2021
OB concepts
  • Job satisfaction
  • Four-Drive theory (Bond, Defend)

Dropbox makes cloud storage and collaboration tools for teams that work from different places, and in 2020 it announced it was becoming a "Virtual First" company (Dropbox, 2020). Virtual First is the name of its culture, and remote work is the default for individual work, not the exception. To support that, Dropbox changed its offices into Dropbox Studios, which are only used for collaboration and community, and it set core collaboration hours so teams in different time zones still overlap, with employees planning the rest of their day around that (Dropbox, 2020).

This was based on research, including a study Dropbox commissioned that found knowledge workers were more focused at home and just as engaged as before, along with its own surveys showing nearly 90% of employees felt productive at home and did not want a rigid five-day office week (Dropbox, 2020). Dropbox did not go with a regular hybrid model because that can create two different experiences, where people in the office get ahead while remote workers fall behind. Instead, the culture is maintained by keeping Studios for group work only and focusing on results instead of hours worked, and a year later the model was still in place, with Studios reopened in San Francisco, Austin, and Dublin (Somers, 2021).

This matters for Dropbox because it uses the same type of work it sells, so it understands its customers better, and it also helps with hiring because jobs are location agnostic, which CEO Drew Houston said was a big advantage for recruiting (Somers, 2021). It connects to job satisfaction because employees feel better about their job when it fits what they value, like work-life balance and not having a long commute, and it also connects to Four-Drive theory, where Studios and core hours support the Bond drive by giving people time to connect and work together, while avoiding a two-tier system supports the Defend drive because it is more fair. For the future of work, Dropbox shows that flexibility works better when it is planned out as a system, not just left up to each person to figure out.

Dropbox. (2020, October 13). Dropbox goes Virtual First. Dropbox Blog. https://blog.dropbox.com/topics/company/dropbox-goes-virtual-first

Business Article: Hybrid Still Isn't Working

Source and type
Recent business article. Harvard Business Review
Author
P. Cappelli and R. Nehmeh
Date
July 1, 2025
OB concepts
  • Expectancy theory
  • Equity theory

Cappelli and Nehmeh (2025) wrote in Harvard Business Review that hybrid work is not working in a lot of companies, not because flexibility itself is the problem, but because managers are still managing hybrid teams the same way they managed everyone in the office. The article is based on interviews with 720 employees at a financial services company, across 38 offices in three countries, plus other recent research (Cappelli & Nehmeh, 2025).

They found a few main issues, starting with new hires having a harder time learning because they are not around experienced staff as much, while helping coworkers gets pushed aside because people are measured on their own key performance indicators (KPIs). At the same time, virtual meetings get bigger and less useful, and promotions tend to reward individual output over teamwork. Their fix is not to bring everyone back to the office, but to set clear rules for meetings and response times, use anchor days where everyone is in together, and build performance reviews that actually reward helping others (Cappelli & Nehmeh, 2025).

This connects to expectancy theory because motivation depends on effort leading to performance, performance leading to a reward, and the employee actually valuing that reward, so if only individual KPIs are rewarded, there is not much reason to spend time helping someone else, even if the company says teamwork matters. It also connects to equity theory because employees compare what they put in and what they get out with other people, so if being seen in the office leads to better promotions than working remotely, that feels unfair, and employees may do less, push for more, or leave. For the future of work, the point is simple: flexibility is not just about how many days people are in the office, and companies have to change how they manage if they want hybrid work to actually work.

Source

Cappelli, P., & Nehmeh, R. (2025, July 1). Hybrid still isn't working. Harvard Business Review. https://hbr.org/2025/07/hybrid-still-isnt-working

Personal Example

OB concepts
  • Job satisfaction
  • Expectancy theory

From winter 2024 to spring 2026, I worked in sales for a custom home builder, where I managed a showhome every weekend on a team that was disorganized, with no mentor to learn from and very little interaction with team members. I only spoke with the owner two or three times, and most of the work was busywork and cold calls that led to nothing. I also had no flexibility, since I had to come into an empty showhome to make calls I could have made from home, and taking time off was hard to afford. All of that lowered my job satisfaction, which is how positive an employee feels about their job, and I was disengaged because the work did not fit what I valued.

Reward was a problem too, because I would get a fixed commission if I took a lead to the closer and it closed, and while that amount motivated me a lot, I only received it one time. I was put in a location that was not set up for success, so a lot of it was out of my control, which connects to expectancy theory, since my effort did not reliably lead to performance, and performance did not reliably lead to a reward. I used the free time at that job to practice videography in the showhome and plan my own business, and I have now worked full time for myself for a little over six months, while also contracting for a media company where the owner mentors me and helps me build proper systems. Now what I sell is what I earn, and if something is not working, I can fix it myself.

Traditionally, the previous generation followed a more set path, where you went to school, got a job, and got going, and you often stayed in that job even if you were not satisfied. My generation is more willing to leave a job that does not fit and build a different path, including self-employment, because flexibility and satisfaction matter more in that decision, not just pay and security. My experience shows that change, since I left a set schedule and a set location for work I control, and my satisfaction is much higher because of it.

Skills Summary

Course concepts
  • Goal-setting
  • Self-efficacy
  • Job satisfaction

Goal-setting theory taught me how to set a goal so that I can actually achieve it, because a specific and challenging goal is much easier to work toward than a vague one. Over the last six months, I have used the SMART acronym many times while setting goals for my business, making them specific, measurable, and tied to a timeline, and being able to hit those goals has made me feel like I am on the right track. As my business grows, I will keep using that same approach for client work and for my own targets, so that progress is measured by results instead of just by hours worked.

Self-efficacy has changed the most for me in practice, since it is about believing that you can actually do the task in front of you. When I first started pitching brands, it felt like the scariest thing in the world, and I was not sure anyone would take me seriously. The first time I pitched something and someone bought, I felt like I got over that fear, because I finally had proof that I could do it. Now I pitch different people and brands a couple of times a month, and that confidence will matter even more in the future as I take on bigger jobs and approach larger clients.

Job satisfaction is the third concept I will carry forward, and my experience in my previous paragraph is the reason why. I learned that satisfaction is not just about pay, since the commission in my sales job motivated me but was not enough to keep me engaged when the work had no flexibility, no mentorship, and no real sense of progress. Now that I am building my own business, I want to create the opposite of that for myself and for anyone who works with me in the future, with clear systems, real support, and flexibility where it is possible, so that people actually want to stay and do good work.

AI and Technology

By Noah Steingart

Corporate Culture

One of the most significant effects that artificial intelligence has on corporate culture is that AI can improve workplace productivity and efficiency. AI tools can automate repetitive administrative tasks and analyze large amounts of information at once. This extra value brought by using AI allows employees to spend more time focusing on their strategic responsibilities, creative thinking, and actual problem-solving and value creation for the corporation.

As AI becomes more integrated into daily operations in firms across the globe, businesses may develop a culture that places greater emphasis on innovation, efficiency, and technological adaptability. Employees are going to be increasingly expected to understand and use digital tools as part of their regular responsibilities as an employee.

However, organizations must ensure that productivity improvements do not create unrealistic expectations. If employees are expected to complete a significant and unforeseen amount of work simply because AI makes certain tasks faster, this can contribute to increased workplace pressure and employee dissatisfaction.

It is plausible to assume that corporate culture is going to be impacted heavily because of the introduction of AI into the workplace. The goals and motives of certain workplaces will influence how corporate culture is affected by artificial intelligence in each of these businesses.

Podcast: How AI Is Changing Talent, Not Just Tasks: Rethinking Where Human Judgment Matters Most

Source and type
Podcast episode. HBR IdeaCast (Harvard Business Review)
Host / guest
Adi Ignatius (host); Paula Goldman (guest)
Date
September 17, 2026
OB concepts
  • Job design
  • Transformational leadership
  • Perceptual errors

Summary

In this episode, Salesforce’s Chief Ethical and Humane Use Officer Paula Goldman discusses how artificial intelligence is fundamentally reshaping organizational leadership, talent management, and job design. She is dissecting the idea that AI use in the workplace is limited to automating isolated individual tasks. She argues that treating AI solely as a cost-cutting tool causes companies to miss its strategic potential. Goldman also stresses that managers must redesign corporate entry-level roles and foster internal mobility within the corporation even after the introduction of AI. Lastly, the podcast speaks to the new ethical frameworks that must be introduced and upheld when incorporating one’s own work with generative AI models.

Organizational Behaviour Connection

This episode connects directly to job design, leadership and perceptual errors. As AI absorbs repetitive, routine tasks, human roles shift toward higher task significance requiring managers to redesign roles so entry-level workers still develop competencies rather than feeling alienated and virtually useless in the corporate hierarchy. It also highlights transformational leadership, as leaders must actively guide cultural adaptation and how the firm can effectively use AI while still leading employees and helping them feel useful in the corporation. Lastly, the podcast stresses the need for leaders and employees to be aware of perceptual bias when using AI. In some cases, stakeholders within a company can use AI for information and perceive the information as flawless because they are viewing it with perceptual bias because they believe AI cannot steer them wrong. Implementing ideas proposed by AI without a review by actual human stakeholders is a critical perceptual error.

Scholarly Article: How Artificial Intelligence (AI) Usage Affects Employees’ Workplace Well-Being: A Job Demands–Resources Perspective

Source and type
Peer-reviewed scholarly article. Personnel Review
Author
Y. Zhang, Z. Lai, X. Liu, and H. Sun
Date
April 29, 2026
OB concepts
  • Workplace stress
  • Job satisfaction
  • Personality and motivation

Summary

Drawing on the Job Demands–Resources (JD–R) model, this study investigates the dual-natured impact of workplace AI usage on employee well-being. The authors demonstrate that AI acts as a job resource by automating repetitive workflows and lowering cognitive load on employees. Increasing AI-supported job autonomy can trigger role ambiguity and job insecurity. They further find that individual regulatory focus moderates these effects, where employees with a promotion focus experience less job insecurity from AI adoption than those with a prevention focus.

Organizational Behaviour Connection

This research ties directly to workplace stress and job satisfaction. When AI offloads routine tasks, it increases perceived job satisfaction. However, when employees view AI as a threat to their role stability, it acts as a hindrance stressor that undermines psychological well-being. Furthermore, an employee’s personality and motivational orientation (promotion vs. prevention focus) dictate whether they interpret technological change as a growth opportunity or a career threat.

Future of Work Link

For employees, this study shows that leveraging AI as an analytical assistant can reduce mental fatigue on repetitive work, provided they proactively adapt their workflows. For managers, it demonstrates that simply rolling out AI tools does not automatically improve morale. Organizations must pair AI adoption with clear communication, training, and supportive HR practices to minimize job insecurity and workplace stress.

Source

Zhang, Y., Lai, Z., Liu, X., & Sun, H. (2026). How artificial intelligence (AI) usage affects employees’ workplace well-being: A job demands–resources perspective. Personnel Review, 55(6), 1509–1526. https://doi.org/10.1108/PR-07-2025-0806

Personal Example

OB concepts
  • Job satisfaction
  • Expectancy theory

While operating my own small landscaping and property services business last summer, I handled every administrative task manually, from writing estimates and scheduling weekly lawn routes to drafting marketing flyers and client follow-up messages. Spending endless hours every evening on paperwork and tedious tasks after working physically completing these jobs everyday. This created significant time pressure and mental fatigue which had several issues. Next summer, I plan on integrating AI tools into my daily operations to draft client proposals, organize seasonal cleanup schedules, and brainstorm promotional marketing.

Offloading this routine administrative work should directly improve my job satisfaction, because I should regain control over my schedule and I’m excited to spend my time on high-value client interactions and hands-on field work where my judgment matters. At the same time, it should reinforce expectancy theory in my day-to-day workflow: Because as I invest effort into using AI to respond to leads faster and present cleaner, more professional quotes, that effort can reliably translate into higher close rates and direct financial rewards. With more free time to spend with friends and family.

Compared to previous generations of small-business owners who relied entirely on manual paperwork or had to hire administrative staff just to scale, my generation treats AI as an accessible operational partner. Instead of viewing technology as a replacement for hands-on work, using AI to handle repetitive back-office tasks allows a small-time operation to stay competitive.

Projection

My views on how AI should be used in the workplace as a business owner or as an employee/manager are not shared by everyone. I must be careful when I argue for these types of strategies as to not project my views onto others and disregard their opinions against AI use. Although I believe the use can improve corporate culture I must uphold my desire for strong corporate culture by not projecting these views onto my employees, coworkers or managers.

Workplace Stress Management

By identifying repetitive administrative tasks as avoidable job demands and using AI tools as a resource to streamline estimates and scheduling, I will be able to prevent burnout during peak landscaping seasons. Moving forward, whether I am scaling my own business or working within a larger organization, I will intentionally audit workflows to ensure technology frees up human capacity rather than creating constant tedious distraction.

Perceptual Bias

My perspective on this has grown significantly as I have learned to pair my own practical judgment with AI tools to tackle unfamiliar business tasks. Early on, creating professional marketing materials, client contracts, and structured project plans felt intimidating without a formal design or administrative background. Going forward I can use AI to complete lots of these tasks efficiently while still overseeing how AI is completing it and I still must audit its completed task to confirm it’s up to my standards. Not allowing myself to fully trust AI’s input prevents any perceptual bias. This can help with strong corporate culture because as perception becomes aligned between members of an organization corporate culture is strengthened.

Job Design and Job Satisfaction

This is the third concept I will carry into my future career. My experience managing both field operations and administrative work showed me that employee engagement depends on having control over how work gets done and spending time on meaningful tasks rather than monotonous work. In the future, as a manager or business owner, I will design roles that delegate routine processing to technology while empowering people with the autonomy, mentorship, and decision-making authority needed to stay motivated and do their best work.

Gen Z Expectations

By Caleb McFarlane

Scholarly Article: Gen Z Perceptions and Expectations Upon Entering the Workforce

Source and type
Scholarly peer-reviewed article. European Review of Applied Sociology
Author
A. E. Mărginean
Date
2021
OB concepts
  • Expectancy theory
  • Equity theory

Mărginean (2021) wrote “Gen Z Perceptions and Expectations Upon Entering the Workforce” in the European Review of Applied Sociology. This article took a dive into some of the expectations Gen Z has of their employers, managers and their future careers. Mărginean’s research was based on an online survey of 69 Gen Z participants located in Romania, and the research explored their preferences on workplace environments, expectations in a workplace and career goals when they were to enter the workforce (Mărginean, 2021).

The study identified that Generation Z values financial stability, opportunities to develop and acquire new skills while maintaining a sense of security. One of the most interesting findings was that 94% of the participants from the study indicated that learning and training opportunities were most valuable to them personally. The study also shows that around 74% of those surveyed valued the opportunity to build connections and relationships with fellow peers and co-workers, meanwhile 60% of the individuals preferred a combination of remote and in-person work, rather than completely working remote (Mărginean, 2021).

Based off my understanding from course concepts, this relates to the expectancy theory as employees seem to be more motivated when they believe that their effort will lead to better performance, which will ultimately result in rewards or incentives that they value. Gen Z employees who see opportunities for skill development, training and career advancement may be more motivated than others to put effort into their work. Equity theory can be another organizational behaviour concept that ties into this, because the research suggests that fair pay, recognition, along with job security are important to employees from Gen Z. Employees compare what they can contribute to an organization with what benefits they will gain in return. If an employee feels as if they are not being treated fairly or equally to another colleague, it is common to see their motivation and satisfaction deteriorate.

For future of work, the research Mărginean conducted shows that employers will need to adapt and understand that the younger generation is seeking more than just pay. Gen Z is seeking opportunities to develop personally and professionally. Employers that are willing to provide mentorship, training and fair opportunities amongst everyone will have an easier time attracting and retaining employees from generation Z. By applying equity and expectancy theory, organizations will be capable of creating workspaces where employees will feel motivated and valued.

Source

Mărginean, A. E. (2021). Gen Z perceptions and expectations upon entering the workforce. European Review of Applied Sociology, 14(22), 20–30. https://doi.org/10.1515/eras-2021-0003

Business Article: What Gen Z Wants from Canadian Employers

Source and type
Recent business article. Smith Business Insight, Smith School of Business, Queen's University
Author
E. Ng
Date
January 8, 2026
OB concepts
  • Expectancy theory
  • Maslow's hierarchy of needs

Eddy Ng (2026) wrote in Smith Business Insight that Generation Z has different expectations when entering the workforce, and employers need to understand what these expectations may be in order for employers to have the ability to retain employees from Gen Z. The article goes into detail about how Gen Z employees value things such as job security, mental health, career development opportunities, work-life balance and working for companies that share similar values to themselves (Ng, 2026).

The main point stated was that younger employees are not just seeking a paycheque, but rather they want to feel supported, have opportunities and develop in their careers in an environment where they feel valued. It is evident that employers who are unable to provide these things struggle more to retain employees from Gen Z. Managers need to adapt and develop an understanding of what Generation Z values, that being communication, opportunities to develop and positive feedback. Companies can respond by providing mentorship, creating flexible work arrangements and creating a positive workspace (Ng, 2026).

This connects to expectancy theory as employees are more motivated when they believe that their effort will lead to good performance, and good performance leads to rewards that they personally value. As an example, Gen Z employees have shown the willingness to work harder when they are aware that their contributions can lead to promotions, recognition and other opportunities. There is also a connection to Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, as some employees may want more than just an increase in their salary. They are seeking a sense of belonging, respect and the opportunity for personal growth.

For the future of work, the main point is that employers should not expect every generation to share the same or similar expectations. Businesses should be able to show they are capable of adaptability within their management styles, while still providing opportunities for growth and creating workspaces where the younger generation feels valued. Companies that have already shown this adaptability have a better chance of retaining Gen Z employees.

Source

Ng, E. (2026, January 8). What Gen Z wants from Canadian employers. Smith Business Insight. https://smith.queensu.ca/insight/content/what-Gen-Z-wants-from-Canadian-employers.php

Personal Example

OB concepts
  • Expectancy theory
  • Leadership

One personal connection that connects to Gen Z workplace expectations was my time working at Flynn Construction. During my employment which took place over the spring and summer of 2023 I worked as an intern assisting in project management. I felt management had high expectations which is understandable as Flynn Construction is a successful and well-known company. Although I understood that having high expectations is part of working in a professional environment, I was often left feeling as if there was a lack of support, communication or guidance from upper management and colleagues to reach those set expectations. Being very new often referred to as “green” in the industry, I was still learning how to go about operations and would have most certainly benefited from more mentorship and feedback on projects I was working on from the more experienced colleagues. This experience connects into the expectancy theory, which explains why employees are more motivated when they believe that their effort will lead to good performance which results in rewards, which in my case would have been recognition. When employees are expected to perform at a high level without proper support or training, they are often left feeling unmotivated, again relating this to my situation, as these feelings are very accurate as to how I felt. Another concept is leadership, as managers need to provide direction, communicate effectively and help employees develop rather than expecting results. My experience showed me specifically, that Gen Z employees want to be challenged and given responsibility, but they also seek employers who are willing to take the time to allow them to develop. I do believe there needs to be a balance between high expectations and providing employees with the adequate tools, guidance and support they need.

Skills Summary

Course concepts
  • Goal-setting
  • Self-efficacy
  • Team development

Goal-setting theory has played a big role in my baseball career playing for Okanagan College because improving as a player requires setting specific goals and consistently working towards them. Whether I was focusing on improving my contact while hitting, which will result in my batting average increasing, or becoming more agile and improving my fast twitch muscles to help my defensive reads in the outfield, I have learned that setting clear goals gives me something to work towards. Instead of me stating that I want to become a better baseball player, setting specific goals allows me to measure my progress over time. Playing college baseball has taught me that achieving goals takes discipline, commitment and consistent effort, this is important because results do not appear overnight which some people forget. I will continue to use the goal-setting theory throughout my career, both in sports and in the workplace, having clear objectives will help me stay motivated while being able to measure my improvement.

Self-efficacy is another concept that has been a factor in my baseball career because it relates directly to my ability to be able to perform a skill successfully. During my baseball career at the college, I’ve faced numerous situations where confidence is very beneficial, these situations include taking an at bat or making a play in the outfield. Over the years I’ve learnt that confidence comes from repetition, preparation and practice. There are times I have made mistakes or have felt unhappy with my play, but being able to move forward and trust my abilities have allowed me to become a better baseball player. Playing a college sport has shown me that having confidence in yourself is important, but increasing your confidence also comes from putting in the time and dedication. If I can use the same mindset when it comes to my professional career, being confident when taking on different responsibilities, I will improve through experiences.

Teamwork and group development have been some of the biggest life lessons I’ve learnt through playing baseball, whether in high school or college. Playing on multiple different teams has allowed me to play with many different people who each individually have different personalities. I have learnt that personal skill can only take a team so far; if a team has the most skilled players but poor chemistry, the team is bound to come up short of the end goal, which I have experienced throughout my playing career. Throughout a baseball season teams face adversity, but being able to work through those situations is critical for the team’s success. This connects to the stages of team development as teams need time to develop relationships, establish roles, like who will be a leader of the team, the person who will step-up and bring the team together. I can carry these lessons into my future workplace because being successful in the business world requires similar concepts such as collaboration and communication and the ability to work with different personalities to reach the end goal.
